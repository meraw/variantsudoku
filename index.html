#020 — Leapfrog

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Leapfrog clues (invented): a frog sits on each green lily pad outside the grid. It jumps onto the first cell of that row or column. Then it keeps hopping forward, away from its pad, by as many cells as the digit it is standing on, until it hops out of the grid. The number on the pad is the sum of the digits the frog lands on.

Example: a row reading 2 7 5 1 9 3 8 6 4 from the pad. The frog lands on the 2, hops 2 cells to the 5, hops 5 cells to the 6, then hops 6 cells and leaves the grid. The clue would be 2 + 5 + 6 = 13.

Setter's note

A new outside clue for the gallery, and as far as I know a new rule. A frog hops along a row, each hop as long as the digit it lands on, and the clue adds up the digits it touched. There are 14 lily pads and nothing else: no givens, and every pad is needed for a unique solution.

The one thing to notice. The digits the frog lands on are its hop lengths, so the clue is simply how far the frog travels. It starts on the first cell and leaves the grid somewhere past the ninth. So: - a total of 9 means its last hop ends exactly one step past the far edge. 17, the largest possible, means it lands on the far cell and that cell is a 9; - if the frog's last landing is the c-th cell from its pad and holds d, then c + d = total + 1. A 16 ends on an 8 in the far cell or on a 9 in the cell before it; - a 9 next to a pad only fits a total of 9.

How it was set. No target grid. A script offered every total still possible at every free pad position and placed the one that taught my solver the most. I first allowed givens whenever no pad made much progress. Those runs ended with 14–18 pads plus 1–2 givens. With givens allowed only if no pad could make progress at all, two runs finished with 14 pads and no givens. I kept the one with the friendlier opening and the cleaner second-solver check. Two runs restricted to the bottom and left edges stalled and were abandoned.

Hint (light spoiler): row 1 has a frog at each end. The 16 on the right can only finish on an 8 or a 9 near the left end. Then follow the left-hand frog.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (39 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests). Rangsk's SudokuSolver, given each pad as an automaton over its row or column, finds the same unique solution and solves it with pointing, a tuple and one contradiction test. Its automaton view is weaker than reading the clue properly (see the verification report). I rate it 4/5. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#019 — Unlucky for Some

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Every clue in this puzzle is 13.

Killer cages: the digits in a cage sum to 13 and do not repeat within the cage.

Little killers: a 13 outside the grid with an arrow gives the sum of the digits along that diagonal. Digits may repeat on a diagonal where sudoku rules allow it.

X-sums: a 13 outside the grid without an arrow (italic: above column 4, right of row 6, below column 1) is an X-sum. Look along that row or column from the clue. The first digit you see is X, and the first X digits, that one included, sum to 13.

Setter's note

This run fell on Friday 13 November, so every number in the puzzle is a 13. There are three killer cages, three little killers and three X-sums, nine 13s in all. There are no givens. Each of the nine clues is needed: take any one away and the puzzle has more than one solution.

How it was set. Usually the hard part of setting is choosing the numbers. Here the numbers were decided before I started, and I could only choose where the 13s go. A new script tried every spot where a 13 could still go: every small cage, every diagonal of three or more cells, every X-sum position or sandwich line. It placed the one that told my solver the most, and stopped as soon as the grid was forced. Seven of eight runs finished with 9–10 clues; one stalled. I didn't use any of the runs with sandwich sums, because a sandwich 13 and an X-sum 13 look identical outside the grid. I kept the run with three of each kind.

Facts worth finding: - An X-sum of 13 is short. X can't be 1 or 2, and X = 5 would need at least 5+1+2+3+4 = 15. So the first digit is a 3 (followed by a pair summing to 10) or a 4 (followed by {1,2,6} or {1,3,5}). - A 13 on a long diagonal is very small. Count the smallest total each diagonal can have, box by box.

Hint (light spoiler): the 13 in the top-right corner covers eight cells. Its smallest possible total is 11, not 8. Now look at the cage it passes through.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (25 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests). Rangsk's SudokuSolver, given all three rules natively, finds the same unique solution and solves it with clue checks, one tuple and singles. I rate it 3/5. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#018 — Flat-Pack City

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Jigsaw sudoku: place 1–9 once each in every row, every column and every bold-outlined region. The regions are irregular: each one is a 3×3 box with jigsaw-puzzle tabs and slots.

Skyscrapers: each digit is the height of a building. A number outside the grid gives how many buildings you can see from that side, looking along that row or column. A building hides every shorter building behind it. Example: seen from the left, the row 3 1 5 2 9 … shows 3, 5 and 9, so that is three buildings.

Setter's note

A city that arrives in a flat box: the regions are jigsaw pieces, and the only clues are ten skyscraper counts around the edge. There are no givens. It's the first jigsaw in this gallery.

The pieces. Each region is a 3×3 box with a tab sticking into one neighbour and a slot where another neighbour's tab fits. I searched for every way to give the boxes tabs (1 or 2 cells deep) so that each region keeps nine connected cells. At most 10 of the 12 inner edges can carry a tab, and I picked a layout that is mirror-symmetric left to right. The tabs climb the outer columns and the middle column slides down.

Facts worth finding: - Law of leftovers. The top three rows hold the top three pieces, apart from the long tab that hangs into rows 4–5 of the middle column. Two tabs from below poke into row 3 instead, so those two cells hold the same pair of digits as the hanging tab. The same works at the bottom, and for the left and right stacks: r2c4 = r8c3 and r2c6 = r8c7. - A skyscraper 7 means only two buildings are hidden, so the low digits are pushed far away from that clue.

How it was set. I drew the pieces first, then tried partners for them with no target grid: region-sum lines (which care about region shapes, so they seemed the natural partner), German whispers, thermometers and outside clues. The lines all needed 3–5 givens or gave easy, random-looking puzzles. Skyscraper clues, chosen one at a time by how much they let my solver deduce, finished with ten clues and no givens. Every clue but one is needed for uniqueness, and the remaining one (the 4 above column 5) is needed to avoid guessing.

Hints (light spoilers): - Start with the 7 at the top right. Where can the 9 of row 1 be, and which piece does that cell belong to? - Row 4 has clues at both ends, 6 and 4. Where is its 9?

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (51 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing; the law of leftovers does real work in 10 of them). Independently, Rangsk's SudokuSolver finds the same unique solution and solves it logically, using a finned X-wing, a few region forcing chains and one contradiction test along the way. I rate it 4/5, crunchy. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#017 — Factor Tree

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Factor lines (red): for every two neighbouring cells along a red line, one digit is a factor of the other (it divides it exactly). For example 2–8, 3–9, 6–3 and 1–7 can be neighbours, but 4–6 and 2–3 can't. A digit may repeat on a line where sudoku rules allow it. The trunk and the six branches are separate lines. Each branch starts on the trunk cell it grows from.

Little killers: a number outside the grid, with an arrow, gives the sum of the digits along that diagonal. Digits may repeat on a diagonal where sudoku rules allow it.

Setter's note

In school maths a factor tree splits a number into its factors, branch by branch. This one is a fir tree drawn in factor lines: a trunk down column 5 and three pairs of diagonal branches. The only other clues are six little killers around the edge. There are no givens.

Factor lines (from the Logic Masters "Sudoku Variants Series") and little killers are both new to this gallery. Little killers were the plan back in run 4, but my solver couldn't check them then. Now it can solve this puzzle, and Rangsk's solver gives a second opinion.

Facts worth finding: - 1 divides everything, but 5 and 7 can only sit next to a 1 on a factor line. They are the lonely primes. 9 can only touch 1 or 3. - The trunk is 8 cells in one column. Only 16 orders work, and each starts with a lonely prime and a 1 at one end. So the cell above the trunk, the star on top of the tree, must be the other lonely prime. - Swapping every 4 with 8, or every 5 with 7, keeps every factor line happy. The little killers are what tell them apart.

How it was set. I drew the tree first and never changed it. Then I chose little killers one at a time from the sums still possible, with no target grid: each time, the one that let my solver deduce the most. Several runs finished with six clues and no given. I kept the only one with clues on all four sides. Two of the branches aren't needed for uniqueness. I kept them because a tree with branches missing on one side looks broken. The verification report lists which marks are needed for what.

Hints (light spoilers): - The 6 and the 24 are three-cell diagonals inside one box. - The 14 is a long diagonal with a very small total. Which trunk cell does it cross, and what does that do to the trunk's order?

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (21 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests). Independently, Rangsk's SudokuSolver, given each factor line as a pairwise rule plus an automaton of the line's valid fills, finds the same unique solution and solves it with tuples, pointing and sum checks. I rate it 3/5, a gentle one for the season. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#016 — Compass Rose

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Arrows: the digits along an arrow sum to the digit in its circle. All eight arrows start from the same central circle, and each arrow on its own sums to that circle.

Sandwich sums: a number outside the grid gives the sum of the digits between the 1 and the 9 in that row or column.

Setter's note

The first arrows in this gallery. Eight arrows point out from one circle in the centre, one per compass direction. There are no other marks inside the grid, no givens, and eight sandwich clues around the edge, like bearings.

The rose is the break-in. Every arrow starts in box 5, so the eight first cells and the circle are 1–9 between them. If the circle weren't 9, the 9 would be at the start of some arrow, and that arrow would sum to more than its circle. So the centre is 9, and every outer cell is 9 minus the box-5 cell beside it. Row 5 and column 5 now have their 9 for the sandwich clues.

How it was set. I drew the compass first and then tried three partners for it. More arrows, grown by the computer or placed in rotation-symmetric sets, needed 2–5 givens. German whispers needed 2–5 givens and ran over the rose. Sandwich clues, chosen one at a time from the values still possible (no target grid), finished with eight clues and no given in one of five runs. That's this puzzle. Every sandwich clue is needed. Three of the arrows (east, south-west, west) aren't needed for uniqueness, only for a guess-free path. I kept them because a compass with gaps isn't a compass.

Hints (light spoilers): - Find the centre digit first (see above). - A sandwich of 33 contains almost every digit from 2 to 8. Which one is missing, and where can it go? - The 4 under column 9 leaves very little room between that column's 1 and 9.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (62 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests). Independently, Rangsk's SudokuSolver (both rules native) finds the same unique solution and solves it logically with basic steps plus one TUVWXYZ-wing and one region forcing chain. That's crunchy, but well short of Pinwheel Whispers. Rated 4/5. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#015 — Newton's Cradle

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Pendulums (invented rule, slate lines): each slate line hangs from a circle. The circled digit is the pendulum's resting point. Read the line away from the circle. The digits swing alternately above and below the circled digit (never equal to it), and every swing is strictly smaller than the one before: each digit is closer to the circled digit than the previous digit was. The first swing may go up or down.

Example: circle 5, then 9, 2, 6 is a pendulum: swings of 4 up, 3 down, 1 up. Circle 5, then 9, 2, 8 is not (the third swing, 3, is not smaller than the second, 3), and neither is 5, 9, 7 (two swings up in a row).

Setter's note

A new line rule, drawn as the desk toy. Five pendulums hang from row 1, and the left ball is lifted and ready to swing (the diagonal line). A second, irregular bar of pendulums hangs from row 6. The rule is a damped pendulum: the digits swing from one side of the resting digit to the other, and each swing is smaller than the last. As far as I know the rule is new.

Facts worth finding: - A pendulum's digits are always all different, so it works like a little cage. - Pendulums are short. With 4 cells the circle must be 3–7, and a 6-cell pendulum can't exist at all. - A 4-cell pendulum with circle 7 must end 9, 6. With circle 3 it must end 1, 4. - The rule is symmetric under swapping every digit d for 10 − d, so the puzzle needs a given to choose between twin solutions. One given (r2c4) does that. The other given (r4c8) is not needed for uniqueness, but makes it solvable without guessing.

How it was set. I drew the cradle first. Five hanging (straight) 4-cell pendulums side by side are impossible, so the lifted ball is part of the design, not decoration. Letting the computer grow pendulums in the lower half gave scattered pictures, so I tried a second bar of pendulums hanging from row 6 instead. I sampled bars, kept those with few solutions, then tested every pair of givens that makes the solution unique. 18 of 2,418 pairs are solvable without guessing, and I took the one with the longest solve whose givens are not in circles.

Hints (light spoilers): - Row 1 has five circles of 4-cell pendulums. Which five digits must they be? - Look at the 6 in row 4, and ask what a circle 7 would need at the bottom of its line.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (40 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests). Independently, Rangsk's SudokuSolver (given each pendulum as an automaton) finds the same unique solution and solves it logically with almost nothing beyond singles. I rate it 3/5: steady rather than crunchy. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#014 — Carry the One

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Sums (invented rule, red symbols): some rows and columns carry two red symbols, a + and an =, on their box borders. Read such a row (left to right) or column (top to bottom) as three three-digit numbers, one per box. It must be a correct sum, with the symbols read in order: - + then = means first + second = third; - = then + means first = second + third.

A symbol sitting on the border between two cells of a row belongs to that row; one between two cells of a column belongs to that column.

Example: a row reading 2 1 8 | 3 4 9 | 5 6 7 and marked + then = is correct, because 218 + 349 = 567.

The only given digits are 1s.

Setter's note

Every marked row or column is the old puzzle "ABC + DEF = GHI with each digit once", because a sudoku row already uses each digit once. As far as I know, using it as a sudoku constraint on whole rows and columns is new. It has 336 solutions, and they all share some lovely facts:

- The answer's three digits always sum to 18 (so the two numbers being added sum to 27). - There is exactly one carry, either out of the units column or out of the tens column, never both, and never out of the hundreds. - The answer starts with 4 or more.

Each of these takes one line to prove (casting out nines), and together they are most of the solve.

How it was set. First I asked the computer how many rows and columns can be sums at once: 8 seemed to be the most. Then I tried patterns I liked. Rows 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 with columns 1, 5 and 9 turned out to be amazingly tight: of the 256 ways to place the symbols, only 2 have any solution at all, with exactly 3 grids each. A single given 1 then makes the solution unique. But neither of my solvers can solve it from there without guessing, so I didn't publish that version. Three 1s make it fair, hence the title. The 1 in row 2 is the one that makes the solution unique. The other two are there so you can solve it without guessing. The row-1 sum is the one clue that isn't strictly needed (both the solution and the logic survive without it); I kept it because it completes the pattern.

Hints (light spoilers): - Look at the 1 in row 3. It is the last digit of that row's answer, so the last digits of the two numbers being added (r3c3 and r3c6) must make 11. That's the carry, so the tens column has none. - Where a marked row meets a marked column, the cell is a digit of both sums. Combine what each sum says about it.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (35 rounds of tier-2 logic, each step one sum against the candidates, no guessing and no contradiction tests). Independently, Rangsk's SudokuSolver, given each sum as an automaton, solves it logically with line checks, two tuples and singles. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#013 — Zip It

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Zipper lines (purple): digits an equal distance from the centre of a purple line sum to the same total. On a line with an odd number of cells (every zipper here has 3 or 5 cells), that total is the digit in the centre cell. Example: a five-cell line 2 – 6 – 9 – 3 – 7 works: 2 + 7 = 6 + 3 = 9, the centre.

Dutch whispers (orange): neighbouring digits along an orange line differ by at least 4.

Lines may cross. Each line keeps its own rule.

One given digit.

Setter's note

Two line types new to this gallery, zipper lines and Dutch whispers, drawn as a zip fastener. The orange chain down column 5 is a single Dutch whisper. Purple teeth cross it on the even rows, and longer purple teeth grow out of it on the odd rows, alternating left and right like interlocking teeth. Short orange stitches sit in the fabric on either side.

Facts worth finding: - A short tooth's centre is the sum of two different digits, so it is at least 3. That leaves very few ways to fill the chain. I counted 72 orders for a whole-house Dutch whisper, and only 24 of them put 3 or more on all four tooth centres. - On a Dutch whisper, 5 can only sit next to 1 or 9, 4 next to 8 or 9, and 6 next to 1 or 2. - The one given is not there to make the solution unique (the lines alone already do that). It is the way in.

How it was set. I drew the fastener myself: the chain, the teeth, and their alternation. I proved some alternatives impossible along the way (teeth on the odd rows, a second full-column whisper). The rest came from solver-guided growth with a fallback given. I then curated it by hand: I replaced two bent diagonal lines with straight or L-shaped ones, and made sure no purple line touches another purple line, so you can always see where a zipper ends. One prettier draft, with orange half-rows facing every long tooth, was unique but couldn't be solved without heavy guessing. It is in the setter's log. The short tooth on row 2 is the one redundant line: it completes the picture but adds no information.

Hints (light spoilers): - Start at the given 4 and read along row 5. - Once the middle of the chain is known, the teeth on rows 4 and 6 have very few totals left.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (35 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing, no contradiction tests) and independently with Rangsk's SudokuSolver, which knows both line types natively. It solves the puzzle with 2 contradiction tests and one forcing chain, about the level of Pinwheel Whispers. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#012 — Are We There Yet?

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Odometers (invented rule, blue lines): every blue line starts in a circle. Travel along the line from the circle to the arrowhead and add up the size of every step, i.e. the difference between each pair of neighbouring digits on the line. The step out of the circle counts too. This total distance must equal the digit in the circle.

Example: a line reading 5 – 4 – 2 – 4 from its circle travels 1 + 2 + 2 = 5, so the circle may hold 5. A line 7 – 5 – 2 – 3 – 4 travels 2 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 7. ✓

Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow.

No given digits.

Setter's note

A new invented line: the odometer. The circle is where the trip starts, and the circle's digit is how far you travel, step by step, along the line. As far as I know this rule hasn't been used before. Its nearest relatives are arrows (a circle that totals its line) and whisper-type lines (rules about neighbouring differences).

Facts worth finding (checked by enumerating every digit sequence): - Every tip is even. The total distance has the same parity as (circle − tip), and the total is the circle, so the tip is even. Each arrowhead in the grid is a 2, 4, 6 or 8. - Short lines double. A two-cell odometer reads c – 2c. A three-cell line c – x – e has e = 2x when x < c, and e = 2c or 2(x − c) when x > c. - Budgets are tight. A circle holds at most 9, so a long line has to travel gently. If all of a line's cells see each other, six of them already need a distance of at least 5. Nine such cells allow exactly one sequence: 9-8-7-6-5-4-3-1-2. - No twin. Replacing each digit d by 10 − d keeps every step the same size but changes the circle, so the rule breaks that symmetry by itself. That's why this puzzle needs no givens. My Fibonacci lines (007) and the renban/kropki puzzle (008) both needed givens just to break a mirror-image twin.

How it was set. There was no target grid. A script drew random routes of 5–8 cells and kept those that at least one sampled grid satisfies. It scored them by how much my logical solver could then deduce, and committed one of the best after a CP-SAT check (the "open-solution" method from run 5, now with odometers). Six such runs gave five layouts with no givens at all (9–10 lines each). I picked this one for its small case lists: with every odometer step capped at 300 fills, only it and one 10-line layout still solve, while the other three get stuck. Every line is needed for uniqueness. The layout came from the script, not from my hand. I like how the two seven-cell hooks in boxes 3 and 9 mirror each other.

Hints (light spoilers): - Mark every arrowhead even first. - Where does box 3's 1 go? Every cell of box 3 is on a line. Try putting a 1 on the column-9 line or on the hook, and see how much distance that costs. - The hook in box 9 behaves a lot like the one in box 3.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver: 35 rounds of tier-2 logic, no guessing and no contradiction tests. Rangsk's SudokuSolver can now read the rule as an automaton (NFA) line. It independently confirms the unique solution, but it leans on many contradiction tests, because its line filter doesn't know that cells of a line sharing a box must differ. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#011 — Skyline Sums

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

X-sums (plain numbers outside the grid): read the row or column from the clue. The first digit you meet is X; the clue is the sum of the first X digits, counting that first digit itself. Example: a row starting 3 – 5 – 1 – … has X-sum 3 + 5 + 1 = 9.

Skyscrapers (numbers in grey boxes): each digit is a building of that height. Looking along the row or column from the clue, a building is visible unless a taller one stands in front of it. The clue is the number of visible buildings. Example: 2 – 5 – 3 – 7 – 1 – 9 – … shows 2, 5, 7 and 9, so its clue would be 4.

The skyscraper clues are: below columns 3, 6 and 8; to the right of rows 2, 4 and 5; to the left of row 6. Every other clue is an X-sum. Rows and columns without a clue have no extra constraint.

No given digits.

Setter's note

Two established clue types that both care about the first digit you meet from the edge. An X-sum tells you how far to count from that digit, and a skyscraper clue tells you how often the view gets taller. Neither rule had been used in the gallery yet. Skyscrapers in particular were new to my toolkit, which now handles both with an exact pass along the whole row or column.

How it was set. As in Betwixt (009), there was no target grid. A script sampled grids that fit the clues so far, listed the totals still possible at each outside position, and scored every candidate clue by how much my logical solver could then deduce. Clues went in as 180° pairs. Left alone, the scoring picked X-sums almost every time, because they simply say more per clue: all four unbiased runs that finished gave X-sum puzzles with at most one skyscraper. So skyscraper moves had their score multiplied by 2.2, and this run came out balanced, with seven of each. Then I pruned by hand: - Row 5's left clue was "X-sum 45", which only says r5c1 = 9. It was a given in disguise, and the puzzle didn't need it. - The skyscraper 3 above column 2 also turned out to be removable.

That leaves 12 clues (7 skyscrapers, 5 X-sums). Eleven are needed for uniqueness. The twelfth (column 5's 32 from below) is needed for a solve without trial and error. The picture is no longer perfectly symmetric.

Hints (light spoilers): - Column 5 has an X-sum at both ends, and 37 + 32 is far more than 45. The two counted stretches must overlap, and the overlapping cells are counted twice. How big must that overlap be, and how long can each stretch be? - Column 4's 8 is tiny: the first digit can only be 2 or 3. - Seven visible buildings (row 5 from the right, column 6 from below) means the line climbs almost every step. Where can the 9 be, and what does that do to the cells just before it?

Logical solvability: verified with two independent solvers. My candidate-elimination solver needs no guessing and no contradiction tests (35 rounds of tier-2 logic). Rangsk's SudokuSolver, fed only the exported puzzle, also solves it. It uses nothing beyond X-sum and skyscraper re-evaluations, tuples and pointing. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#010 — Product Placement

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Bridges (invented rule, teal lines): each line connects two circled end cells. The product of the two circled digits equals the sum of all the digits on the line between them. Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow.

Examples: a three-cell bridge 2 – 6 – 3 works (2 × 3 = 6). A four-cell bridge 2 – 1 – 5 – 3 works (2 × 3 = 1 + 5).

Given digits: 2.

Setter's note

A new invented line: on a bridge, the two circled ends multiply to give the sum of everything in between. Multiplication doesn't respect any digit relabelling, so unlike my Fibonacci lines (007) or the renban/kropki puzzle (008), there's no built-in twin solution. The givens here carry real information; they don't just break a symmetry.

Facts I found with small scripts before setting: - A three-cell bridge whose cells all see each other (one row, column or box) must be 2 – 6 – 3 or 2 – 8 – 4 in some order of the ends. Every such bridge has a 2 at one end. - A 1 at one end makes the middle equal to the other end. A three-cell bridge can only do that when its middle cell and that far end don't see each other. - If the inner cells of a bridge fill a whole 3×3 box, they sum to 45, so the ends must be 5 and 9. - A bridge can never fill an entire row: the ends a and b would need (a + 1)(b + 1) = 46 = 2 × 23. - Nine three-cell bridges, one in every box, were never satisfiable in my tests (0 of 40 random layouts, 0 of 300 symmetric ones). Four or five usually are.

How it was set. The first plan was Seven Bridges, after Euler's Königsberg problem: four killer cages as the land masses and exactly seven bridges joining them in Euler's pattern. It didn't work. A random grid satisfies a given bridge about 1–3% of the time, so each bridge carries only about 5–7 bits, against roughly 72 needed for a unique sudoku. Seven bridges plus four cages still needed eight givens. A version with a river (a slow thermometer for each river arm) had no solution at all in the geometry I drew. Both are in the setter's log.

What worked was a designed break-in, a 45-rule bridge. The teal serpentine winds through all nine cells of the centre box from r5c3 to r5c7, so its ends are 5 and 9. Around it, a script placed bridges in 180° pairs without any target grid. Each candidate line had to be satisfiable (sampled grids, then CP-SAT) and was scored by how much my logical solver could then deduce. When no line helped enough, a given became allowed. Four runs gave 2–4 givens; this one needed only two. Afterwards one bridge turned out to be redundant (its symmetric partner does the work), so I removed it and the symmetry breaks in that one corner. Every remaining bridge and given is needed: without any of them except one bridge, the puzzle has more than one solution. Without that bridge (r9c8–r7c9) it stays unique, but the logic then needs contradiction tests.

Hints (light spoilers): - Start at the 4. Its bridge lies entirely inside box 4, so 4 × (one end) = (the middle). - Then look just above: a bridge whose ends share a row and a box, but whose middle sits in another box. - The serpentine's ends are 5 and 9, which gives the 5 and 9 of box 5 only rows 4 and 6.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests; the biggest steps are case lists of 54 and 106 fills on the two six-cell bridges). Rangsk's SudokuSolver can't model this invented rule, so there's no second solver check. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#008 — Good Fences

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Renban (pink lines): each line holds a set of consecutive digits, in any order, with no repeats.

Kropki (white dots): the digits in two cells separated by a white dot are consecutive (they differ by 1).

Negative constraint: all white dots are shown. Two orthogonally adjacent cells with no dot between them are never consecutive.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A classic ruleset this time, as a counterweight to the invented Fibonacci lines: renban lines plus white kropki dots with the negative constraint. The two rules pull against each other. A renban wants consecutive digits close together. An undotted edge is a fence that keeps consecutive digits apart.

Where they meet: - Three renban cells in a straight orthogonal row can't all have fences between them. The middle digit would need to be non-consecutive with both neighbours, and no set of three consecutive digits allows that. - The two L-shaped renbans have no dots at all, so each holds five consecutive digits snaking around a corner with no two neighbours on the line consecutive. - The diagonal renban through the centre has no fences between its own cells, because they only touch at corners. The dots and fences around it still restrict it.

Why there are four givens. Replace every digit d by 10 − d and consecutive digits stay consecutive, so every renban and every dot still works. Without givens the puzzle therefore always has at least two solutions. Here it has exactly two, and the second is the first turned upside down. The givens are placed as 180°-symmetric pairs whose digits sum to 10 (8 and 2, 4 and 6), so the picture stays symmetric. Each given is needed for the logical path, though not for uniqueness.

How it was set. I drew the renbans first, then asked CP-SAT for grids that satisfy them while having at most twelve consecutive neighbour pairs, in a 180°-symmetric pattern. In such a grid the dots are not my choice: the negative constraint means every consecutive pair must be marked. I kept a grid whose dots and renbans left only the twin pair of solutions. Then I added symmetric given pairs, guided by the logical solver. My first layout also had two short vertical renbans at the right and left. I removed them: with dots on both steps, a straight three-cell line is already forced to be consecutive, so they added nothing. The method is close to the one I used for Which Way Up (a symmetric negative constraint, then givens to break the symmetry). The rules and the logic are different, but I want to say so.

Hints (light spoilers): start at the 8 in row 2. Both of its neighbours are dotted, so they are 7 and 9. Now look at the top-left L, which contains one of them. If that cell were 9, the L would need 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. Its two cells in row 2 could then only be 5 and 6, which are consecutive and have no dot between them. The bottom-right L mirrors this with the 2.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). Its two biggest steps were the L renbans, where only three fills survive once the fences are taken into account. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#007 — Liber Abaci

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Fibonacci lines (gold, invented rule): read each line starting from the end with the dot. From the third cell on, every digit is the last digit of the sum of the two digits before it.

Example: 3 4 7 1 8 works: 3+4 = 7, 4+7 = 11 (last digit 1), 7+1 = 8.

A 0 can never be placed, so two neighbouring digits that are followed by a third digit on the line never sum to 10. Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow it.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A new invented line this time, named after Fibonacci's book of 1202. A Fibonacci line works like the famous sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …, except that only the last digit is kept, and the line may start from any two digits. The first two digits on a line fix all the others.

Things worth knowing about the rule (these are deductions, not extra rules): - Parity. The last digit of a sum has the same parity as the sum, so odd + odd = even, odd + even = odd and so on. Every line of three or more cells is therefore either all even or follows odd, odd, even repeating, starting at some point in that cycle. Combine this with "every row, column and box has exactly four even digits". - Length. At most six cells of a line fit in one row, column or box (only eight sequences of six different digits work). The two long lines here are staircases that leave their boxes. - Fourth cells. If a line starts a, b, then the third digit is a+b and the fourth is a+2b (last digits only). - The line lengths in the grid are 3, 5 and 8. Those are Fibonacci numbers too.

Why there are four givens. Multiply every digit in a valid grid by 3 and keep the last digit: 1→3, 2→6, 3→9, 4→2, 5→5, 6→8, 7→1, 8→4, 9→7. Every Fibonacci line still works, because the rule only uses addition and last digits. The same is true for ×7 and ×9. So Fibonacci lines on their own can never give a unique puzzle: every solution comes with three relabelled copies, and a given 5 can't tell them apart (5 maps to itself). I searched line layouts with CP-SAT until the lines alone left exactly four solutions, which is the minimum possible. In other words, the lines do all the work except choosing the labels. Then I added givens. One given would be enough to make the solution unique, but with a single given my logical solver gets stuck almost at once (I tested every single-given option on several layouts). So the four givens are there for a fair path. They come in two 180°-symmetric pairs (9 and 1, 7 and 3), like the lines. Take away any one and the puzzle stays unique, but the solver then needs trial-and-error.

How it was set. Layout first: five 180°-symmetric pairs of lines, orthogonal steps only, lengths 3, 5 or 8. I annealed the layout on CP-SAT solution counts. Then I added symmetric pairs of givens one at a time, always the pair that helped my logical solver most, and finally removed any pair it could do without. Every pair of lines matters. Remove one and the puzzle either has more solutions or needs trial-and-error.

Hints (light spoilers): start with the staircase through the 9 in box 4 (it starts at r6c1). Parity first: the 9 is in fourth place, so the line can't be all even, and its 1st, 4th and 7th cells are odd. Next, the fourth digit is a + 2b, so the first two digits must give a + 2b ending in 9. List those seed pairs and check each against the box and row it runs through. The staircase in the other half of the grid works the same way with its 1.

Spoiler (read after solving): opposite cells of the finished grid always sum to 10. The rules don't force this. It falls out of the symmetry: turning the grid round and multiplying by 9 gives the same grid back.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). The most work it needed on a single line was 25 seed pairs. It has not been test-solved by a human.

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Seesaws (brown lines, invented rule): each brown line is a seesaw resting on its circled cell, the fulcrum. Every other digit on the line pushes its side down with a force equal to the digit × its distance from the fulcrum, counted in cells along the line. The cells next to the fulcrum count ×1, the next ones ×2, and so on. Every seesaw balances: the total on the left of the fulcrum equals the total on the right. The digit in the fulcrum cell is not part of either total.

Example: a line reading 9 (F) 1 4 balances, because 9×1 = 1×1 + 4×2.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A new line type this time, and no killer cages. A seesaw is like an arrow or a cage with weights. A digit far from the fulcrum counts double or triple, so a small digit at the end of a long arm can balance a big digit close to the middle. Another way to put it: the fulcrum sits exactly at the line's centre of mass. Every row has one seesaw, and all nine fulcrums stand in column 5, stacked like a spine down the middle of the grid. The lines are rotationally symmetric (row 1 mirrors row 9, row 2 mirrors row 8, and so on).

How it was set. I didn't grow the clues one by one this time. I chose the shape first: one straight seesaw per row, every fulcrum in column 5, 180° symmetry. Then I searched the arm lengths, with CP-SAT counting solutions for each layout. Seesaws are very restrictive. Nine of them alone leave only two solutions, and the layout above is the one I found that gets there. I can't call two solutions a puzzle, and the empty grid gives a human no foothold either: my logical solver couldn't place a single digit. So I added givens, keeping the ones that helped the logic most, and then removed any given the logic could do without. Four remained. To be precise about "needed": one given would be enough for uniqueness, but take away any one of the four and my logical solver gets stuck. They're there for a fair path, not for the count.

Some lines are much tighter than others. Take rows 6 and 8: the short arm (×2, ×1) has to balance a long arm (×1, ×2, ×3, ×4), so the short-arm digits are big and the long-arm digits small. Rows 2 and 4 are the same lines reversed.

Hints (light spoilers): start in row 2. On the right side, the most the two cells can contribute is 9×2 + 8×1. Compare that with the left side, which already has the 6 at ×2. Row 3 works the same way with the 7 at ×4. Then use the boxes to pass digits between the rows. The bottom row is a nice case analysis once a few small digits are known.

#001 — Speed Dial

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Keypad lines (purple): two cells that are next to each other along a purple line must hold digits that are orthogonally adjacent on a standard keypad:

1 2 3
4 5 6
7 8 9

So 5 can sit next to 2, 4, 6 or 8; 2 can sit next to 1, 3 or 5; 1 can sit next to only 2 or 4. (A computer numpad has 7 8 9 on top, but the adjacencies are identical, so either picture works.) Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow it.

Setter's note

This is the first puzzle in the collection and it tries out a new line type. On a keypad, digits that touch alternate between odd and even (the corners and the 5 are odd, the edge digits are even). That means every keypad line alternates parity, and parity is where the puzzle starts.

How it was set. I began with the zigzag across rows 1–2. It puts five cells of the same parity into row 1, and a row only has four even digits, so those five cells have to be odd. That fixes the odd/even pattern of the top two rows, and it also means the zigzag uses every digit 1–9 exactly once. In other words, it traces a path through the keypad that visits all nine keys without lifting your finger. The spiral in box 5 works the same way: centre and corners odd, edges even, and the whole box is another such path. I added the side zigzags, the two short verticals and the row-7 line, and the solver showed the line layout alone left only 240 grids. Then I used a logic-guided search to choose the givens.

Why three givens? The keypad can be rotated or mirrored, giving 8 symmetries in all, and none of them break the adjacency rule. So any solution of the lines alone comes in a family of 8 relabelled grids. One given can never separate all 8, which makes two givens the theoretical minimum. Two-given versions existed, but none could be solved by my logical solver without guessing. Three givens keep it clean.

Hints (light spoilers): count the parities. Box 2 and column 5 soon tell you the parities on the row-7 line. After that, look at which keys a corner digit is allowed to touch.
