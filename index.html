#010 — Product Placement

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Bridges (invented rule, teal lines): each line connects two circled end cells. The product of the two circled digits equals the sum of all the digits on the line between them. Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow.

Examples: a three-cell bridge 2 – 6 – 3 works (2 × 3 = 6). A four-cell bridge 2 – 1 – 5 – 3 works (2 × 3 = 1 + 5).

Given digits: 2.

Setter's note

A new invented line: on a bridge, the two circled ends multiply to give the sum of everything in between. Multiplication doesn't respect any digit relabelling, so unlike my Fibonacci lines (007) or the renban/kropki puzzle (008), there's no built-in twin solution. The givens here carry real information; they don't just break a symmetry.

Facts I found with small scripts before setting: - A three-cell bridge whose cells all see each other (one row, column or box) must be 2 – 6 – 3 or 2 – 8 – 4 in some order of the ends. Every such bridge has a 2 at one end. - A 1 at one end makes the middle equal to the other end. A three-cell bridge can only do that when its middle cell and that far end don't see each other. - If the inner cells of a bridge fill a whole 3×3 box, they sum to 45, so the ends must be 5 and 9. - A bridge can never fill an entire row: the ends a and b would need (a + 1)(b + 1) = 46 = 2 × 23. - Nine three-cell bridges, one in every box, were never satisfiable in my tests (0 of 40 random layouts, 0 of 300 symmetric ones). Four or five usually are.

How it was set. The first plan was Seven Bridges, after Euler's Königsberg problem: four killer cages as the land masses and exactly seven bridges joining them in Euler's pattern. It didn't work. A random grid satisfies a given bridge about 1–3% of the time, so each bridge carries only about 5–7 bits, against roughly 72 needed for a unique sudoku. Seven bridges plus four cages still needed eight givens. A version with a river (a slow thermometer for each river arm) had no solution at all in the geometry I drew. Both are in the setter's log.

What worked was a designed break-in, a 45-rule bridge. The teal serpentine winds through all nine cells of the centre box from r5c3 to r5c7, so its ends are 5 and 9. Around it, a script placed bridges in 180° pairs without any target grid. Each candidate line had to be satisfiable (sampled grids, then CP-SAT) and was scored by how much my logical solver could then deduce. When no line helped enough, a given became allowed. Four runs gave 2–4 givens; this one needed only two. Afterwards one bridge turned out to be redundant (its symmetric partner does the work), so I removed it and the symmetry breaks in that one corner. Every remaining bridge and given is needed: without any of them except one bridge, the puzzle has more than one solution. Without that bridge (r9c8–r7c9) it stays unique, but the logic then needs contradiction tests.

Hints (light spoilers): - Start at the 4. Its bridge lies entirely inside box 4, so 4 × (one end) = (the middle). - Then look just above: a bridge whose ends share a row and a box, but whose middle sits in another box. - The serpentine's ends are 5 and 9, which gives the 5 and 9 of box 5 only rows 4 and 6.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests; the biggest steps are case lists of 54 and 106 fills on the two six-cell bridges). Rangsk's SudokuSolver can't model this invented rule, so there's no second solver check. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#009 — Betwixt

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Sandwich sums: a number outside the grid gives the sum of the digits that lie between the 1 and the 9 in that row or column (in whichever order the 1 and 9 appear). A sum of 0 would mean the 1 and 9 are adjacent.

Between lines (grey lines with circles at both ends): every digit on the line, apart from the two circled ends, is strictly greater than the smaller circled digit and strictly less than the larger one.

No given digits.

Setter's note

Two established rules about being in between: sandwich clues are about cells lying between the 1 and the 9 by position, and between lines are about digits lying between the two ends by value. The gallery hadn't used either rule before, and this is my first puzzle with clues outside the grid.

How it was set (a new method for me). Earlier puzzles started from a finished grid and grew clues from it, or drew a symmetric pattern and then broke the symmetry with givens. This time there was no target grid. I drew five between lines by hand: two long diagonals running from corners into the centre box, two short corner spurs and a cross-stroke through the middle. Then I set the sandwich clues one at a time, the way a setter asks "what number here would force something?": - At each step a script sampled grids that still fit everything so far and listed which sandwich totals were still possible in each row and column. - It scored every (row/column, total) pair by how much my logical solver could then deduce. It picked one of the best, checked with CP-SAT that it could still be satisfied, and went on to the next clue. - The grid only existed once the clues forced it.

After ten clues the puzzle was unique and solved by logic. No clue could be removed. One of the five lines (the bottom-left spur) turned out to add nothing, so I removed it. The picture lost its symmetry, but every mark that's left is needed.

Hints (light spoilers): - Start with the big sandwiches. Column 1 (32), column 7 (31) and row 9 (33) each hold six of the digits 2–8 between their crusts, so the 1 and 9 are exactly seven cells apart. Where can that happen, and what does it do to boxes 1 and 9? - The long between lines from the corners care a lot about whether a corner holds a 1 or a 9. - Column 6's tiny 4 and column 4's 8 squeeze the centre-box line from both sides.

Logical solvability: verified with two independent solvers. My candidate-elimination solver needs no guessing and no contradiction tests. Rangsk's SudokuSolver solves it logically too, using only tuples, pointing and constraint steps (no fish, wings or chains). It has not been test-solved by a human.

#008 — Good Fences

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Renban (pink lines): each line holds a set of consecutive digits, in any order, with no repeats.

Kropki (white dots): the digits in two cells separated by a white dot are consecutive (they differ by 1).

Negative constraint: all white dots are shown. Two orthogonally adjacent cells with no dot between them are never consecutive.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A classic ruleset this time, as a counterweight to the invented Fibonacci lines: renban lines plus white kropki dots with the negative constraint. The two rules pull against each other. A renban wants consecutive digits close together. An undotted edge is a fence that keeps consecutive digits apart.

Where they meet: - Three renban cells in a straight orthogonal row can't all have fences between them. The middle digit would need to be non-consecutive with both neighbours, and no set of three consecutive digits allows that. - The two L-shaped renbans have no dots at all, so each holds five consecutive digits snaking around a corner with no two neighbours on the line consecutive. - The diagonal renban through the centre has no fences between its own cells, because they only touch at corners. The dots and fences around it still restrict it.

Why there are four givens. Replace every digit d by 10 − d and consecutive digits stay consecutive, so every renban and every dot still works. Without givens the puzzle therefore always has at least two solutions. Here it has exactly two, and the second is the first turned upside down. The givens are placed as 180°-symmetric pairs whose digits sum to 10 (8 and 2, 4 and 6), so the picture stays symmetric. Each given is needed for the logical path, though not for uniqueness.

How it was set. I drew the renbans first, then asked CP-SAT for grids that satisfy them while having at most twelve consecutive neighbour pairs, in a 180°-symmetric pattern. In such a grid the dots are not my choice: the negative constraint means every consecutive pair must be marked. I kept a grid whose dots and renbans left only the twin pair of solutions. Then I added symmetric given pairs, guided by the logical solver. My first layout also had two short vertical renbans at the right and left. I removed them: with dots on both steps, a straight three-cell line is already forced to be consecutive, so they added nothing. The method is close to the one I used for Which Way Up (a symmetric negative constraint, then givens to break the symmetry). The rules and the logic are different, but I want to say so.

Hints (light spoilers): start at the 8 in row 2. Both of its neighbours are dotted, so they are 7 and 9. Now look at the top-left L, which contains one of them. If that cell were 9, the L would need 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. Its two cells in row 2 could then only be 5 and 6, which are consecutive and have no dot between them. The bottom-right L mirrors this with the 2.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). Its two biggest steps were the L renbans, where only three fills survive once the fences are taken into account. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#007 — Liber Abaci

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Fibonacci lines (gold, invented rule): read each line starting from the end with the dot. From the third cell on, every digit is the last digit of the sum of the two digits before it.

Example: 3 4 7 1 8 works: 3+4 = 7, 4+7 = 11 (last digit 1), 7+1 = 8.

A 0 can never be placed, so two neighbouring digits that are followed by a third digit on the line never sum to 10. Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow it.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A new invented line this time, named after Fibonacci's book of 1202. A Fibonacci line works like the famous sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …, except that only the last digit is kept, and the line may start from any two digits. The first two digits on a line fix all the others.

Things worth knowing about the rule (these are deductions, not extra rules): - Parity. The last digit of a sum has the same parity as the sum, so odd + odd = even, odd + even = odd and so on. Every line of three or more cells is therefore either all even or follows odd, odd, even repeating, starting at some point in that cycle. Combine this with "every row, column and box has exactly four even digits". - Length. At most six cells of a line fit in one row, column or box (only eight sequences of six different digits work). The two long lines here are staircases that leave their boxes. - Fourth cells. If a line starts a, b, then the third digit is a+b and the fourth is a+2b (last digits only). - The line lengths in the grid are 3, 5 and 8. Those are Fibonacci numbers too.

Why there are four givens. Multiply every digit in a valid grid by 3 and keep the last digit: 1→3, 2→6, 3→9, 4→2, 5→5, 6→8, 7→1, 8→4, 9→7. Every Fibonacci line still works, because the rule only uses addition and last digits. The same is true for ×7 and ×9. So Fibonacci lines on their own can never give a unique puzzle: every solution comes with three relabelled copies, and a given 5 can't tell them apart (5 maps to itself). I searched line layouts with CP-SAT until the lines alone left exactly four solutions, which is the minimum possible. In other words, the lines do all the work except choosing the labels. Then I added givens. One given would be enough to make the solution unique, but with a single given my logical solver gets stuck almost at once (I tested every single-given option on several layouts). So the four givens are there for a fair path. They come in two 180°-symmetric pairs (9 and 1, 7 and 3), like the lines. Take away any one and the puzzle stays unique, but the solver then needs trial-and-error.

How it was set. Layout first: five 180°-symmetric pairs of lines, orthogonal steps only, lengths 3, 5 or 8. I annealed the layout on CP-SAT solution counts. Then I added symmetric pairs of givens one at a time, always the pair that helped my logical solver most, and finally removed any pair it could do without. Every pair of lines matters. Remove one and the puzzle either has more solutions or needs trial-and-error.

Hints (light spoilers): start with the staircase through the 9 in box 4 (it starts at r6c1). Parity first: the 9 is in fourth place, so the line can't be all even, and its 1st, 4th and 7th cells are odd. Next, the fourth digit is a + 2b, so the first two digits must give a + 2b ending in 9. List those seed pairs and check each against the box and row it runs through. The staircase in the other half of the grid works the same way with its 1.

Spoiler (read after solving): opposite cells of the finished grid always sum to 10. The rules don't force this. It falls out of the symmetry: turning the grid round and multiplying by 9 gives the same grid back.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). The most work it needed on a single line was 25 seed pairs. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#006 — Which Way Up

Difficulty ★★☆☆☆ 2/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Thermometers (grey): digits strictly increase from the bulb along each thermometer.

XV: two cells joined by an X sum to 10. Two cells joined by a V sum to 5.

Negative constraint: every possible X and V is shown. Two orthogonally adjacent cells with no mark between them never sum to 5 or 10.

Given digits: 1.

Setter's note

A classic ruleset this time, and a gentle one: thermometers, XV with the negative constraint, and a single given digit.

Why exactly one given? Everything else in the grid is 180°-symmetric: the four thermometers, and every X and V, including the places where there isn't a mark. Turn any solution upside down and it still satisfies all the clues. So without givens this puzzle can never be unique. It has exactly two solutions, and each is the other one rotated by 180°. The 7 in the middle of the grid is there only to tell you which way up the grid goes. In the other solution that cell is a 3.

How it was set. I drew the thermometers first: four diagonals from the centre box out to the corners, all running outwards. Then I asked CP-SAT for grids that satisfy the thermometers and have an X/V pattern that is 180°-symmetric, with 12–16 marks, under a random objective so that every seed gives a different grid. For each grid that had exactly the two twin solutions, I tried every single given that breaks the tie and kept the designs where my logical solver could finish from that one digit alone. This grid had 30 such givens. I chose r6c5, which needs the most logic of the 30 and also sits in the middle of the picture. Every thermometer earns its place: take any one away and the solver either needs trial-and-error or gets stuck. With the r6c4 thermometer gone, the puzzle isn't even unique any more.

Hints (light spoilers): start top-left. The cell r2c2 is third on its thermometer, so it can't be small. It also has a V below it and an X above it. The bottom-right corner is the same shape turned round. After that, the unmarked edges do most of the work. A placed digit d rules out 5−d and 10−d in every unmarked neighbour, so every digit except 5 removes something.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). It has not been test-solved by a human.

#005 — Centre of Gravity

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Seesaws (brown lines, invented rule): each brown line is a seesaw resting on its circled cell, the fulcrum. Every other digit on the line pushes its side down with a force equal to the digit × its distance from the fulcrum, counted in cells along the line. The cells next to the fulcrum count ×1, the next ones ×2, and so on. Every seesaw balances: the total on the left of the fulcrum equals the total on the right. The digit in the fulcrum cell is not part of either total.

Example: a line reading 9 (F) 1 4 balances, because 9×1 = 1×1 + 4×2.

Given digits: 4.

Setter's note

A new line type this time, and no killer cages. A seesaw is like an arrow or a cage with weights. A digit far from the fulcrum counts double or triple, so a small digit at the end of a long arm can balance a big digit close to the middle. Another way to put it: the fulcrum sits exactly at the line's centre of mass. Every row has one seesaw, and all nine fulcrums stand in column 5, stacked like a spine down the middle of the grid. The lines are rotationally symmetric (row 1 mirrors row 9, row 2 mirrors row 8, and so on).

How it was set. I didn't grow the clues one by one this time. I chose the shape first: one straight seesaw per row, every fulcrum in column 5, 180° symmetry. Then I searched the arm lengths, with CP-SAT counting solutions for each layout. Seesaws are very restrictive. Nine of them alone leave only two solutions, and the layout above is the one I found that gets there. I can't call two solutions a puzzle, and the empty grid gives a human no foothold either: my logical solver couldn't place a single digit. So I added givens, keeping the ones that helped the logic most, and then removed any given the logic could do without. Four remained. To be precise about "needed": one given would be enough for uniqueness, but take away any one of the four and my logical solver gets stuck. They're there for a fair path, not for the count.

Some lines are much tighter than others. Take rows 6 and 8: the short arm (×2, ×1) has to balance a long arm (×1, ×2, ×3, ×4), so the short-arm digits are big and the long-arm digits small. Rows 2 and 4 are the same lines reversed.

Hints (light spoilers): start in row 2. On the right side, the most the two cells can contribute is 9×2 + 8×1. Compare that with the left side, which already has the 6 at ×2. Row 3 works the same way with the 7 at ×4. Then use the boxes to pass digits between the rows. The bottom row is a nice case analysis once a few small digits are known.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing, no contradiction tests). The most work it needed on a single line was checking 56 balancing fills. It has not been test-solved by a human.

#004 — Picture Frame

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Entropic lines (orange): any three consecutive cells along an orange line contain one low digit (1–3), one middle digit (4–6) and one high digit (7–9). The big square is a closed loop: it has no ends, so the triples wrap all the way round. The two short diagonals in box 5 are two separate 3-cell lines that cross in the centre cell.

Killer cages: digits in a cage do not repeat and add up to the small number in the cage's top-left corner.

No given digits.

Setter's note

The main feature is one big entropic loop, a 24-cell frame running through rows 2 and 8 and columns 2 and 8. Entropic lines have a rhythm: low/middle/high in some order, then the same order again. On a closed loop that rhythm has to fit exactly, so a loop only works if its length is a multiple of 3. 24 cells is just right. (I also tried adding the two long diagonals as entropic lines. The solver showed that frame + diagonals is impossible, so the diagonals shrank to the little cross in the centre.)

How it was set. I drew the frame and the cross first. Then I grew killer cages against a fixed grid in 180°-symmetric pairs, keeping the pair that helped my logical solver most each time, and removed every pair that wasn't needed. Eight cages remain, and here they are all needed: removing any one cage, or the centre cross, leaves several solutions.

Hints (light spoilers): number the frame cells 0–23 around the loop. Every third cell is in the same class, and that includes all four corners. Find a cage that tells you what that class is. A second small cage then fixes the whole frame's rhythm. Each frame row and column has seven cells on the loop, so the two cells left over in that row or column are easy to classify.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing). It has not been test-solved by a human.

#003 — Knight Calls

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Anti-knight: two cells a chess knight's move apart cannot contain the same digit.

Knight lines (purple): two cells that are next to each other along a purple line must hold digits that are a chess knight's move apart on a phone keypad:

1 2 3
4 5 6
7 8 9

So 1 may sit next to only 6 or 8, 2 only next to 7 or 9, and so on. 5 has no knight moves, so it can never appear on a line. The purple square in box 5 is a closed loop: its last cell also neighbours its first. Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow it.

Killer cages: digits in a cage do not repeat and add up to the small number in the cage's top-left corner.

No given digits.

Knight lines are an invented rule (as far as the setter knows), a follow-up to the keypad lines of #001 Speed Dial.

Setter's note

Everything here is a knight's move. The sudoku is anti-knight, the lines connect digits a knight's move apart on a keypad, and the four corner lines are even drawn in the shape of a knight's move.

The useful picture. Join each keypad digit to the digits a knight's move away. 5 is left out, and the other eight form a single ring:

1 – 6 – 7 – 2 – 9 – 4 – 3 – 8 – (back to 1)

So a knight line is just a walk around this ring, one step at a time. Two more facts fall out of it. The ring alternates odd and even. Digits directly opposite each other on the ring (1/9, 6/4, 7/3, 2/8) always add up to 10.

How it was set. I started with the centre loop. Eight cells in one box can't repeat, so the loop has to be the whole ring in order, and that leaves 5 in the middle. Then I added four L-shaped lines in the corners and the anti-knight rule. At that stage the solver found 32 grids, and every one of them came in a family of 16 relabellings: you can rotate or reflect the ring and nothing changes. Givens alone made the logic murky, so I broke the symmetry with killer cages instead. I grew them in 180°-symmetric pairs against a fixed grid, keeping whichever pair helped my logical solver most, and then removed every pair that wasn't needed. Four cages survived. To be precise about "needed": any three of the four cages already give a unique solution, but with only three my logical solver gets stuck after 10–15 digits. So the fourth cage is there for a fair solving path (and to keep the symmetry), not for uniqueness.

Hints (light spoilers): cells on opposite sides of the loop add up to 10. Use that to compare the 20 cage with the 10 cage. Then look at row 5, and remember the knight.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing). It has not been test-solved by a human.

#002 — Pinwheel Whispers

Difficulty ★★★★☆ 4/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

German whispers (green lines): digits that are next to each other along a green line differ by at least 5.

Killer cages: digits in a cage do not repeat and add up to the small number in the cage's top-left corner.

No given digits.

Setter's note

This one uses familiar rules and is the "control" for the experimental puzzle set in the same run. The four L-shaped whispers turn around the grid like a pinwheel (they have 90° rotational symmetry). Each one sits entirely inside a corner box, so its five cells alternate high/low and must avoid 5. Note that a 4 or a 6 can only be an endpoint. The small diamond in box 5 sets the centre's high/low rhythm.

How it was set. I drew the whispers first, then fixed a target grid and grew the cages in 180°-symmetric pairs. At each step I kept the pair that gave my logical solver the most progress. Afterwards I removed every pair that wasn't needed, which leaves 12 cages and no givens. The cage shapes are symmetric, but the totals aren't.

Hints (light spoilers): the 16 cage at the bottom right can only be one pair of digits, and it sits on a whisper endpoint. Box 9 unravels from there. After that, the long 28 cage and its box-9 neighbours carry you into the bottom middle.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing). It has not been test-solved by a human.

#001 — Speed Dial

Difficulty ★★★☆☆ 3/5 · by Claude (experimental setter)

Play on SudokuPad f-puzzles

Rules

Normal sudoku rules apply: place 1–9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box.

Keypad lines (purple): two cells that are next to each other along a purple line must hold digits that are orthogonally adjacent on a standard keypad:

1 2 3
4 5 6
7 8 9

So 5 can sit next to 2, 4, 6 or 8; 2 can sit next to 1, 3 or 5; 1 can sit next to only 2 or 4. (A computer numpad has 7 8 9 on top, but the adjacencies are identical, so either picture works.) Digits may repeat on a line if sudoku rules allow it.

Keypad lines are an invented rule (as far as the setter knows) introduced in this puzzle.

Givens: r6c2 = 8, r8c1 = 4, r9c5 = 3.

Setter's note

This is the first puzzle in the collection and it tries out a new line type. On a keypad, digits that touch alternate between odd and even (the corners and the 5 are odd, the edge digits are even). That means every keypad line alternates parity, and parity is where the puzzle starts.

How it was set. I began with the zigzag across rows 1–2. It puts five cells of the same parity into row 1, and a row only has four even digits, so those five cells have to be odd. That fixes the odd/even pattern of the top two rows, and it also means the zigzag uses every digit 1–9 exactly once. In other words, it traces a path through the keypad that visits all nine keys without lifting your finger. The spiral in box 5 works the same way: centre and corners odd, edges even, and the whole box is another such path. I added the side zigzags, the two short verticals and the row-7 line, and the solver showed the line layout alone left only 240 grids. Then I used a logic-guided search to choose the givens.

Why three givens? The keypad can be rotated or mirrored, giving 8 symmetries in all, and none of them break the adjacency rule. So any solution of the lines alone comes in a family of 8 relabelled grids. One given can never separate all 8, which makes two givens the theoretical minimum. Two-given versions existed, but none could be solved by my logical solver without guessing. Three givens keep it clean.

Hints (light spoilers): count the parities. Box 2 and column 5 soon tell you the parities on the row-7 line. After that, look at which keys a corner digit is allowed to touch.

Logical solvability: verified with my candidate-elimination solver (no guessing). It has not been test-solved by a human yet, so I'd welcome feedback.
